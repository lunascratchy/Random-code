Why the Pi turned off

The buck converter's output dips when the motors start

The Pi runs from a buck converter on the same LiPo as the motors. When four motors start at once, they draw a spike of current, the LiPo voltage dips, and the buck's 5 V output falls with it. The Pi's log stops mid-line with no shutdown message, which is what an instant power loss looks like. A Pi 5 is less tolerant of this than older Pis.

It hadn't happened before for two likely reasons. The bench test started all four motors at a fixed PWM from rest. And a battery that's partly drained sags further under the same spike. The new firmware and test script both ramp the motors up now, and Phase B adds capacitors to cover the rest.

About the 5 A setting. There is a Pi 5 setting for this: PSU_MAX_CURRENT=5000 in the EEPROM, or usb_max_current_enable=1 in config.txt. It only tells the Pi to trust its supply and raises the USB port limit from 600 mA to 1.6 A. It doesn't make the buck deliver any more current, and it can't stop a brownout. The brownout comes from the buck's input voltage dipping, not from the USB limit.

Turn it on only if the buck converter is rated for 5 A or more. If it's rated lower, the Pi will let USB devices draw more than the buck can supply, and it will brown out more easily. Phase C shows how to check and set it.

What was wrong with the driving

Four causes, and what's been done about each

1 · Encoder dropouts · needs your bench test

A rear encoder kept losing its signal

124 NO_MOTION faults in the logs, all at exactly 0 speed, one wheel per session. While the Nano thinks a wheel is stopped, it pushes that side harder (the curve). When the fault latches it stops every motor, then retries about every 2 s (the jerk). The left encoder's B wire was on D13, which has the Nano's LED on it. It's on D12 now in both the wiring and the firmware. Phase D checks that both rear encoders count steadily.

2 · Turning · fixed in firmware

The inner side kept reversing in turns

No wheel may run slower than 600 ticks/s, so a turn either keeps the inner wheel forward (an arc) or reverses it (a spin). Every reversal brakes the whole side, waits up to 800 ms for it to stop, then kicks it again. The old code switched at one exact turn rate, so a turn held near it kept reversing the inner side. Now it switches into a spin at about 0.5 rad/s and only back to an arc below about 0.3 rad/s.

3 · Straight-line drift · fixed in firmware, calibration pending

Left and right didn't get the same wheel speed

The two rear encoders count different ticks per turn (730 and 655), but the mixing added the same number of ticks to both sides. At low speed the sides got 0.86 and 0.92 rev/s, so the robot drifted in an arc. It now mixes in rev/s, and both get 0.92. If 730 and 655 themselves are wrong, it will still curve while odometry says it's straight, which is why Phase E counts them by hand.

4 · Starts and stops · keyboard fixed, minimum speed pending

Teleop sent steps, not ramps

teleop_twist_keyboard sends one step per key event, and its key-repeat delay (about 0.5 s) matched the 0.5 s timeouts, which gave a go-stop-go. The new key_drive.py ramps and sends at a steady 20 Hz. The controller node was already smooth. The remaining jerk is the Nano's 600 ticks/s minimum (about 0.12 m/s): every start jumps to at least that speed. Phase F measures how much lower it can safely go.

Hands-on walkthrough

Step by step: from the bench to smooth teleop

Eight phases, in order. Each step says what to do, why, and what a pass looks like. Don't skip a failed pass: tell me what you saw instead. You'll need your capacitors, a multimeter, masking tape, a marker, a tape measure and a box to lift the robot.

After every motor test, check the Pi's power: run vcgencmd get_throttled. throttled=0x0 means no low voltage since boot. Anything ending in 1, or a value like 0x50000, means the 5 V supply dipped and you need more capacitance or a fuller battery.
Phase A

Make it safe

  1. Stop ROS if it's running (Ctrl+C in the launch terminal).Why: the tests talk to the Nano directly, and only one program can use its serial port.
  2. Shut the Pi down (sudo shutdown now), wait for the green LED to stop flashing, then unplug the LiPo.Why: you'll be connecting capacitors to the battery lines. A short there can damage the pack, the buck converter or the Pi.
  3. Measure the LiPo at rest with the multimeter. Charge it if it's below 11.4 V.Why: a fuller pack sags less when the motors start, so it browns out less. Full is 12.6 V.
  4. Put the robot on a box so all six wheels hang free.Why: the bench tests spin the motors.
Phase B

Add capacitors to ride through the start-up dip

A capacitor stores charge right next to the load and supplies the short spike, so the battery and the buck converter don't have to.

Polarity matters. Electrolytic capacitors (the cylinders) have a stripe marked "−" down one side: that leg goes to ground. Backwards, they can burst. The voltage printed on the capacitor must be higher than the line it sits on.
  1. Buck converter input (battery side): your largest electrolytic, rated 16 V or more (25 V is better), across the buck's IN+ and IN− terminals. 1000–2200 µF is ideal.Why: this is where the dip starts. The capacitor holds the buck's input up during the motor spike.
  2. Buck converter output (Pi side): 470–1000 µF rated 6.3 V or more (10 V is better), across OUT+ and OUT−, as close to the Pi end of the cable as you can.Why: a second buffer right at the Pi's 5 V input. It also covers the voltage drop in the thin wires of the cut USB-C cable.
  3. L298 motor supply: 470–1000 µF rated 16 V or more, across each L298 board's 12 V and GND terminals.Why: the motors then pull their start-up spike from this capacitor instead of the shared battery wires.
  4. If you have small ceramic ones (marked "104", 100 nF): one across each motor's two terminals, if you can solder there.Why: they absorb the electrical noise from the motors' brushes, which can disturb the encoders and the Pi.
  5. If you can, run the buck's input on its own two wires straight to the battery connector, instead of sharing a wire with the L298s.Why: motor current flowing through a shared wire drops its voltage, and the buck sees that drop. Separate wires are rewiring only, no new parts.
  6. Before reconnecting the LiPo, check every capacitor's polarity against the "−" stripe once more.
Phase C

The 5 A setting (only if the buck is rated for it)

  1. Read the rating on the buck converter or its listing. You're looking for "5A" or more of continuous output.Why: the setting tells the Pi it can draw that much. If the buck can't deliver it, the Pi will brown out more often.
  2. If it's 5 A or more, open the EEPROM settings:
    sudo rpi-eeprom-config --edit
    Add this line at the end, save, and reboot:
    PSU_MAX_CURRENT=5000
    Why: with a cut USB-C cable, the Pi can't ask the supply how much it can give, so it assumes the worst and limits all USB ports to 600 mA together. The lidar and the Nano share that limit.
  3. If it's under 5 A, or you can't tell, skip this phase.Why: the Pi didn't shut off because of the USB limit. Skipping this costs nothing for now.
Phase D

Bench test with the wheels in the air

Prove the Pi stays up while the motors run, and both rear encoders count steadily.

  1. Reconnect the LiPo, boot the Pi, and check the Nano is there:
    ls -l /dev/arduino
  2. Unplug the lidar's USB for these tests.Why: it frees up most of the Pi's USB power budget. One less thing that can go wrong.
  3. Start gently, at PWM 80 for 10 s. The script ramps up over 2 s by itself:
    python3 ~/auracle_tools/encoder_check.py drive 80 10
vcgencmd get_throttled
    Why: a low first test is the safest check that the capacitors and soft start keep the Pi alive.
    Pass: the Pi stays on, throttled=0x0, and both sides show a steady positive ticks/s.
  4. Then the full test, at PWM 120 for 20 s:
    python3 ~/auracle_tools/encoder_check.py drive 120 20
vcgencmd get_throttled
    Pass: both sides read about 650–800 ticks/s with no <-- reads ~0 flags, and the throttled value is still 0x0.
  5. Run it again. During the 20 s, gently wiggle each rear encoder connector and cable, one at a time.Why: this recreates driving vibration. A side that drops to 0 when you touch one wire has found the culprit.
    Pass: no side drops to 0.
  6. Test backwards:
    python3 ~/auracle_tools/encoder_check.py drive -120 10
    Pass: both sides read negative.
  7. If the Pi shuts off at any point, stop and tell me at which PWM it happened and your battery voltage.
Phase E

Count encoder ticks per wheel turn

Check the 730 and 655 numbers. If they're wrong, the robot curves while odometry says it's straight.

  1. Unplug the LiPo. Keep the Nano on USB. Put a tape mark on the left rear wheel and a matching mark on the frame.
  2. Start the counter (motors stay off):
    python3 ~/auracle_tools/encoder_check.py count
  3. Turn the left rear wheel forward exactly 10 turns, mark to mark. Read "total L" and divide by 10. Repeat once, then do the right rear wheel with "total R".Why: ten turns spread the error of not stopping exactly on the mark.
    Pass: the two runs on each side agree within about 1%. Write down both results next to 730 and 655.
Phase F

Find the lowest speed the wheels hold on the ground

This sets how gently the robot can start and crawl. Today every start jumps to 600 ticks/s.

  1. Put the robot on the floor with 2 m of clear space in front. Reconnect the LiPo.
  2. Drive forward for 4 s at a time, starting at PWM 100 and going down by 10 each run:
    python3 ~/auracle_tools/encoder_check.py drive 100 4
python3 ~/auracle_tools/encoder_check.py drive 90 4
python3 ~/auracle_tools/encoder_check.py drive 80 4
    Keep going down until a side stalls or stutters.Why: on the ground, the wheels need more power to keep turning than in the air. The lowest PWM that still rolls smoothly gives the real minimum speed.
  3. For the lowest PWM where both sides rolled smoothly, write down the PWM and the ticks/s for each side.Why: I'll set the firmware's minimum speed about 15% above that. A lower minimum means smaller jumps at the start and gentler crawling.
Phase G

First drive with the keyboard

  1. Plug the lidar back in. Terminal 1, start the robot:
    cd ~/auracle_ws/Random-code && source install/setup.bash
ros2 launch auracle_bringup launch_robot.launch.py namespace:=auracle use_joy_node:=false
    Wait until diff_cont, joint_broad and imu_broadcaster have all been activated.
  2. Terminal 2, start the keyboard driver:
    cd ~/auracle_ws/Random-code && source install/setup.bash
ros2 run auracle_bringup key_drive.py --ros-args -r cmd_vel:=cmd_vel_joy -r __ns:=/auracle
    Keep that terminal focused while you drive.
  3. Keys. Hold one at a time:
    w / i forward s / , backward a / j spin left d / l spin right u / o forward arc left / right m / . backward arcs q / z speed level ±10% space stop smoothly
    Why: it starts at 60% speed. Spins and arcs are kept clear of the arc/spin switch point, so the inner side doesn't keep reversing.
  4. Straight-line test: lay 3 m of tape on the floor. Start over one end and hold w to the other end. Measure how far sideways the robot ended up. Do it at 60% and at 100%.
    Pass: less than about 10 cm sideways over 3 m, and no red Nano FAULT lines in Terminal 1.
  5. Turn test: hold a for a full turn, then d. Then hold u and o. Watch whether one side stops while the other drives.
    Pass: both sides keep turning the whole time, with no bouncing and no faults.
  6. Check the Pi's power afterwards: vcgencmd get_throttled.
Phase H

Drive with the controller

  1. Stop the keyboard driver. If the controller is plugged into or paired with the Pi, restart Terminal 1 with use_joy_node:=true:
    ros2 launch auracle_bringup launch_robot.launch.py namespace:=auracle use_joy_node:=true
    If it's on your Windows PC through the UDP bridge, keep use_joy_node:=false and start the bridge as usual.
  2. Controls:
    LT / RT left / right track Back + triggers: reverse Y / A forward / back X / B turn left / right LB + left stick analog drive D-pad ↑/↓ speed level ±10%
    Why: it starts at 50%. The controller node already smooths every input, and the firmware fixes now apply to it too.
  3. Repeat the straight-line and turn tests from G.4 and G.5. For turns, also try a slow arc with LB + stick and gradually push the stick further sideways.
    Pass: the arc tightens smoothly, then becomes a spin once. It doesn't keep switching back and forth.
